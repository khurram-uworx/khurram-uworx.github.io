For over twenty years, I built software without Agile.

Not because we lacked discipline.

Quite the opposite.

Our team was never more than five engineers. The newest guy had already been with us for over ten years.

We had documentation. Real documentation. Word documents, design specs, old-school wikis. We sat together, talked constantly, and knew each other's systems almost as well as our own.

After twenty years, estimation wasn't a ceremony.

It was:

"How long?"

"About a week."

"Make it two. Something always goes wrong."

"Fair."

Done.

Then I left and joined a modern software house doing staff augmentation.

My first backlog meeting arrived.

I opened Jira.

The board alone looked like it required onboarding.

A Product Owner, a Scrum Master, several engineers, ten tickets, and one clear mission:

"Before this meeting ends, we need estimates for everything we're starting on Monday."

The first ticket appeared.

Questions.

Assumptions.

Clarifications.

More questions.

Twenty minutes later we were finally discussing the estimate.

"Okay everyone, Poker."

Cards appeared.

3.

4.

5.

6.

Reveal.

3, 5, 8, 13, 13.

"Why 13?"

"Unknowns."

"Why 3?"

"Looks straightforward."

"Why 8?"

"Somewhere in the middle."

The debate continued.

Then the next ticket.

And the next.

And the next.

An hour later we had numbers.

Whether we had certainty was another question.

What struck me wasn't that the process was wrong.

It was that it was solving a problem my old team never had.

Those engineers were trying to discover, in sixty minutes, what my previous team had learned about each other over twenty years.

Back then, we never played Jira Poker.

Not because we were better engineers.

Because after twenty years together, we already knew what card everyone was holding.







That first backlog meeting left me with a question.

What exactly is a story point?

So I did what engineers do.

I bought the books.

The books were surprisingly reasonable.

Story points, they said, were not time.

They were complexity. Uncertainty. Effort. Risk.

A five-point story wasn't supposed to mean five hours or five days. It simply meant "more than a three, less than an eight."

Interesting.

Actually quite elegant.

Then I went back to work.

"We have five engineers."

"Two-week sprint."

"We need around fifty points."

Wait.

Fifty points?

If points aren't time, why are we calculating capacity with them?

The next sprint came.

Then another.

Soon I could predict the conversations.

"We delivered 47 points."

"We usually deliver 52."

"We should commit to 50."

The books kept saying points were not time.

The spreadsheets seemed to disagree.

The velocity chart certainly disagreed.

The BI dashboard definitely disagreed.

The budget forecasts disagreed with extraordinary confidence.

Everywhere I looked, story points had quietly become a unit of time while everyone continued insisting they were not.

A strange collective agreement.

Like calling a duck a complexity metric while measuring its speed, forecasting its migration patterns, and budgeting next year's ducks based on historical duck throughput.

Nobody was being dishonest.

The people running releases wanted predictability.

The people funding projects wanted forecasts.

The people building dashboards wanted numbers.

And complexity does not fit neatly into charts.

Time does.

So complexity slowly became time, while retaining its original name.

At first I found this frustrating.

Then I remembered something from my previous life.

For twenty years I had watched executives ask for certainty where certainty did not exist.

I had watched project plans transformed into commitments.

Estimates transformed into promises.

Probabilities transformed into deadlines.

None of this was new.

Only the vocabulary had changed.

Eventually I stopped arguing with it.

Not because I agreed.

Because I realized I no longer cared enough.

At some point in a career, many engineers become fascinated by process.

Then architecture.

Then strategy.

Then organizational design.

And then, if they are lucky, they come full circle.

Back to code.

Back to solving problems.

Back to that quiet satisfaction of making a machine do exactly what you intended.

The velocity charts would continue.

The dashboards would continue.

The quarterly metrics would continue.

They always had.

They always would.

Meanwhile, I had a compiler to satisfy.

And unlike the dashboards, it was refreshingly honest.
